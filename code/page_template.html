Where languages come from

Every one of the world's {{counts.spoken|int}} spoken languages in Glottolog, on one map: how they are related, how many are in danger, how many people speak them, and where the great language families may have begun.

Nobody knows exactly how many languages there are, because nobody agrees exactly where one language ends and the next begins. But the best open attempt to list them all, Glottolog, counts {{counts.spoken|int}} spoken first languages, living and dead, and places nearly all of them on a map. It also sorts them into families, the way a biologist sorts species: English sits inside West Germanic, inside Germanic, inside Indo-European, and Indo-European is just one of {{counts.families|int}} families that cannot, on present evidence, be shown to be related to each other.

This page puts that whole tree on a map. Colour the world by family, then click a family to see its branches light up: Indo-European, then Germanic, then West Germanic, then English. Switch to endangerment to see how many languages are slipping away, to speakers to see how few languages most people speak, or to diversity to find the places where languages are packed most densely. A separate layer, clearly labelled, shows where specialists think the great families began.

    02 · Families and isolates{{counts.lineages|int}} separate lineages

    Glottolog groups languages into families only where the evidence of shared descent is solid, which is stricter than many popular charts. On that standard the world's {{counts.spoken|int}} spoken languages fall into {{counts.families|int}} families and {{counts.isolates|int}} isolates: languages such as Basque in Europe, Burushaski in the Karakoram, Nihali in central India and Kusunda in Nepal, with no relatives anyone has been able to prove. Some isolates are only known from writing, like Sumerian. Together that is {{counts.lineages|int}} separate lineages, of which {{counts.lineages_living|int}} still have at least one living language.

    The families are very unequal. Five of them, Atlantic-Congo, Austronesian, Indo-European, Sino-Tibetan and Afro-Asiatic, hold {{counts.top5_share|pc1}} of all languages between them. At the other end, {{counts.families_le5|int}} of the {{counts.families|int}} families have five languages or fewer.

    03 · Where the languages areA belt of diversity

    Languages are not spread evenly. Counting the living languages Glottolog lists in each country, Papua New Guinea comes first with {{countries.top_n.0.n|int}}, then Indonesia ({{countries.top_n.1.n|int}}), Nigeria ({{countries.top_n.2.n|int}}), India ({{countries.top_n.3.n|int}}) and China ({{countries.top_n.4.n|int}}). Papua New Guinea is also the deepest in history: its languages belong to {{countries.top_lineages.0.lineages|int}} different lineages, against {{countries.top_lineages.1.lineages|int}} for Indonesia and {{countries.top_lineages.2.lineages|int}} for Brazil. Relative to land area, Vanuatu is in a class of its own, with {{countries.top_density.0.per10k|f1}} languages per 10,000 square kilometres.

    To compare places fairly I also divided the world into squares of {{grid.cell_km|int}} by {{grid.cell_km|int}} kilometres on an equal-area projection and counted the languages whose point falls in each. The densest square, in {{grid.top_n.0.place|raw}}, holds {{grid.top_n.0.n|int}} living languages; the square with the most separate lineages, in {{grid.top_lin.0.place|raw}} near the border with Indonesia, has {{grid.top_lin.0.lin|int}}. Just {{grid.top1pct_cells|int}} squares, the densest 1% of the world's land, hold {{grid.top1pct_share|pc1}} of all living languages. The densest squares lie in a tropical belt: New Guinea and Melanesia, the Nigeria and Cameroon borderland, southern Mexico, and the eastern Himalaya where India, Myanmar and China meet.

    04 · Languages in dangerMost of the world's languages are endangered

    Glottolog's Agglomerated Endangerment Status combines the assessments of Ethnologue, UNESCO and the Endangered Languages Catalogue into one six-step scale. Only {{aes.safe_pct|pc1}} of spoken languages are rated not endangered. {{aes.pct.threatened|pc1}} are threatened, {{aes.pct.shifting|pc1}} are shifting (children are no longer learning them as a matter of course), {{aes.pct.moribund|pc1}} are moribund and {{aes.pct.nearly extinct|pc1}} nearly extinct. Together that makes {{aes.endangered|int}} endangered languages, {{aes.endangered_pct|pc1}} of the total, or {{aes.endangered_of_living_pct|pc1}} of those still spoken. Another {{aes.extinct|int}} ({{aes.extinct_pct|pc1}}) are already extinct.

    05 · SpeakersA few giants, thousands of small languages

    Glottolog does not count speakers, so for numbers I turned to Wikidata, which holds a count for {{speakers.n|int}} of the {{counts.spoken|int}} spoken languages ({{speakers.coverage_pct|pc1}}). The counts are a patchwork: they come from different years, from {{speakers.before2000|int}} counts made before 2000 to {{speakers.since2015|int}} made since 2015, with {{speakers.n_undated|int}} undated, and some are of first-language speakers only while others include people who speak the language as a second language. Where a language has several counts I took the most recent (the smallest, if several share that date, as that is usually the first-language figure), or the largest if none is dated. Treat them as orders of magnitude.

    Even so, the pattern is unmistakable. The counts add up to {{speakers.total|bn}} billion. The 10 largest languages, from Mandarin Chinese ({{speakers.top.0.n|m}} million) to Japanese, account for {{speakers.top10_share|pc1}} of that, the 20 largest for {{speakers.top20_share|pc1}} and the 100 largest for {{speakers.top100_share|pc1}}. Half the languages with a count have fewer than {{speakers.median|int}} speakers, and those without a count are mostly smaller still. By family, Indo-European languages account for {{speakers.by_family.0.pct|pc1}} of counted speakers and Sino-Tibetan for {{speakers.by_family.1.pct|pc1}}.

    06 · OriginsWhere the great families began

    Every family had a first speech community somewhere. Where, and when, is one of the great detective stories of the human past, pieced together from reconstructed vocabulary, the shape of family trees, archaeology and, lately, ancient DNA. The answers are hypotheses, and some are fiercely contested. The table below sets out the main proposals for {{origins.n_fam|int}} large families, with the places and dates their authors give and a reference for each; the map shows them as stars when you switch on Proposed homelands. For Indo-European, Uralic, Afro-Asiatic and Uto-Aztecan the literature is split, and each main hypothesis is shown.

    Alongside the literature I computed two neutral reference points for every family from Glottolog alone. The present centre is the centre of gravity of where the family's living languages are spoken today. The diversity hotspot is the place where the family's deepest branches crowd most closely together: the language with the most distinct top-level branches within 500 kilometres. That follows an old rule of thumb in linguistics, that a family has usually been longest where its deepest divisions are found side by side.

    How far is each proposed homeland from the family's diversity hotspot and present centre?

    Great-circle distance in kilometres from each proposed homeland to the hotspot of the family's deepest branches (dot) and to the centre of its living languages today (ring).

    For {{origins.n_hot_within500|int}} of the {{origins.n_compared|int}} families the hotspot falls within 500 kilometres of a proposed homeland: Austronesian ({{origins.an_km|int}} km from Taiwan, against {{origins.an_centre_km|int}} km for its present centre in New Guinea), Pama-Nyungan ({{origins.pn_km|int}} km), Quechuan ({{origins.qu_km|int}} km), Bantu ({{origins.bantu_km|int}} km) and the northern Uto-Aztecan proposal. For Indo-European the hotspot lands in the Balkans, where Greek, Albanian, Slavic, Romance and Romani meet, far from both the steppe and Anatolia: three thousand years of later movement have reshuffled Europe. For Sino-Tibetan it lands in north-east India, nearer the minority view of a south-western homeland than the Yellow River. Hotspots are a heuristic, not proof, and where they disagree with the literature, the literature usually has more to go on.

    Read the origins layer with care

    The homelands are hypotheses from the cited studies, not results of this one; the points mark the approximate centre of a proposed region, and the dates are the authors' ranges. Niger-Congo is not accepted as a proven family by Glottolog, so its star stands for a proposal, not for a family on the map. The computed centres depend on where Glottolog puts each language and on which languages survived to be recorded.

    07 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    Languages and families. Glottolog {{meta.glottolog|raw}} (Hammarström, Forkel, Haspelmath and Bank), in its CLDF release, lists {{counts.languoids_language_level|int}} language-level entries. I dropped {{counts.bookkeeping|int}} 'bookkeeping' entries, which are names kept for reference rather than languages, and mapped the remaining {{counts.mapped|int}}. Figures on families, endangerment, diversity and speakers use the {{counts.spoken|int}} spoken first languages; the other {{counts.other_nonfamily|int}} ({{counts.sign|int}} sign languages, {{counts.pidgin|int}} pidgins, {{counts.unclassifiable|int}} unclassifiable, {{counts.unattested|int}} unattested and a few artificial and mixed languages and speech registers) are on the map in grey. A family is a top-level genealogical family in Glottolog; an isolate is a language with no proven relatives. 'Living' means any status other than extinct.

    Endangerment is Glottolog's Agglomerated Endangerment Status (not endangered, threatened, shifting, moribund, nearly extinct, extinct); I count threatened to nearly extinct as endangered. Speakers are Wikidata's 'number of speakers' for items with a Glottolog code, chosen as described above; they cover a minority of languages and mix years and definitions. Diversity uses Glottolog's country lists for each language and an equal-area grid of {{grid.cell_km|int}} km squares (Equal Earth projection); a language counts in the square that holds its point. Country areas are from Natural Earth. Origins: homelands are typed in from the studies listed below; the present centre is the spherical mean of a family's living languages; the diversity hotspot takes the family's top-level branches (splitting further where one branch holds 80% or more of the languages and there are no more than two), and picks the language with the most distinct branches within 500 km.

    What this is not. A dot is one point per language, so the map shows where languages come from more than where their speakers live now: English, Spanish and Portuguese have one dot each, in Europe. The language and dialect line is a judgement, and other catalogues count differently. Endangerment ratings are for whole languages and can be out of date. Homelands are contested, and the map shows proposals, not facts.
